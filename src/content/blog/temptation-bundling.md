You already know what you should do. Go to the gym, clear the inbox, walk the dog in the cold. The problem was never information. The problem is that the thing you should do is dull and its payoff is distant, while the thing you want to do is fun and its payoff is immediate. Temptation bundling is a way of rigging that contest — by attaching the immediate pleasure to the delayed-payoff behaviour, so the boring thing borrows the appeal of the fun one.

It is one of the most under-used techniques in behaviour change, partly because it sounds too simple to work. It isn't. There's a controlled study behind it, a clear mechanism, and a few sharp edges worth knowing before you rely on it.

## What temptation bundling actually is

Temptation bundling means allowing yourself to do something you *want* to do only while you're doing something you *should* do. The "want" is an indulgence you'd otherwise do anyway — often something you might feel mildly guilty about. The "should" is a valuable habit you struggle to start.

Audiobook only at the gym. Favourite podcast only while doing the dishes. Trashy reality show only while folding laundry. The structure is always the same: a guilty pleasure gated behind a productive behaviour.

The term comes from Katherine Milkman, a behavioural economist at Wharton, who coined it after noticing she'd happily go to the gym if it meant she could keep listening to a page-turner novel she'd otherwise binge at home. The indulgence pulled her toward the chore.

This is different from a simple reward. A reward comes *after* the work ("I'll watch an episode once I've been to the gym"). A bundle happens *during* the work, and crucially, the indulgence is restricted to that moment. You don't get the audiobook on the sofa. You only get it on the treadmill.

## The research behind it

In 2014, Milkman, Julia Minson and Kevin Volpp ran a field experiment at the University of Pennsylvania, published in *Management Science*. They gave participants access to four addictive audio-novels — *The Hunger Games* and the like — and split them into three groups.

One group could only listen to the novels at the gym, on iPods locked in gym lockers (full bundling). A second group was encouraged to restrict listening to the gym but kept the iPods themselves (intermediate). A third group simply received a bookstore gift card and no bundling instruction (control).

The full-bundling group visited the gym 51% more often than the control group in the early weeks. The intermediate group landed in between, at around 29% more. The effect decayed over the study — a detail I'll come back to — but at the end, 61% of participants chose to pay to have their gym access bundled with audiobooks going forward. They valued the technique enough to spend money locking themselves into it.

That is the empirical core: a single, well-designed field study showing a real, if fading, increase in a hard habit. It is not a mountain of evidence, and you should treat the exact percentages as specific to one experiment rather than laws of nature. But the mechanism it demonstrates is robust and shows up in everyday experience constantly.

## Why it works: making the "should" attractive

Most habits fail at the moment of initiation, not the moment of effort. Getting to the gym is harder than the workout. The reason is that we're wired to over-weight immediate costs and rewards relative to future ones — a bias behavioural economists call present bias, or hyperbolic discounting. A workout costs you something now (effort, discomfort, time) and pays you later (fitness, health). The brain discounts that later payoff heavily, so the present cost wins and you stay home.

Temptation bundling attacks this directly. It injects an *immediate* reward into the present cost, shifting the moment-to-moment maths. James Clear, in [his framework of the four laws of behaviour change](/blog/4-laws-of-behavior-change), files this under the second law: make it attractive. The behaviour itself doesn't get easier, but the experience of doing it becomes something you look forward to.

There's a second effect that's easy to miss. Bundling solves the indulgence's guilt problem too. If you'd watch the trashy show anyway, doing it while folding laundry converts wasted time into productive time. You stop feeling bad about the indulgence and you get a chore done. Both halves of the bundle improve. That dual benefit is why people who try it tend to keep doing it.

## How to build a bundle

You need two ingredients and one rule.

1. **List your "shoulds."** The valuable habits you keep failing to start — exercise, admin, meal prep, studying, cleaning. Be specific about the moment of initiation, because that's the part bundling fixes.
2. **List your "wants."** The indulgences you reach for automatically — a specific podcast, an audiobook series, a show, scrolling a particular app, a fancy coffee. The stronger the pull, the better the bundle.
3. **Pair one of each, then enforce the restriction.** The want is only allowed during the should. This is the part people skip, and it's the part that matters. If you let yourself have the indulgence elsewhere, the bundle has no leverage — you'll just take the easy version.

A few pairings that work in practice:

| Should (habit) | Want (indulgence) |
| --- | --- |
| Treadmill or stationary bike | Binge-worthy audiobook or podcast |
| Folding laundry / dishes | Favourite TV show |
| Processing email / admin | A specific good coffee or playlist |
| Long walk | Phone call with a friend you rarely catch |
| Stretching / mobility work | The one YouTube channel you love |

Notice that the best pairings are with habits that occupy your hands but leave your attention free, or vice versa. Audio-based indulgences pair beautifully with physical chores. A show pairs with a mindless manual task. Trying to bundle two things that both demand full attention — reading a book while studying — doesn't work.

## How it relates to habit stacking

Temptation bundling is often confused with [habit stacking](/blog/habit-stacking-guide), and they pair well, but they're not the same thing. Habit stacking chains a new habit onto an existing one using the formula "after I [current habit], I will [new habit]" — it solves the *cue* problem by borrowing a trigger you already have. Temptation bundling solves the *attractiveness* problem by borrowing a reward. One gives the behaviour a reliable starting signal; the other gives it appeal.

You can combine them. Milkman's own fuller prescription stacks the two: "After I [stack cue], I will [bundle the want with the should]." For example: *after I pour my morning coffee (cue), I will only drink it while clearing my inbox (bundle).* The stack guarantees you start; the bundle makes starting pleasant. Used together they cover two of the most common failure points in [building a habit that sticks](/blog/how-to-build-habits-that-stick).

## Where temptation bundling breaks

It's a useful tool, not a cure-all. Three failure modes are worth flagging.

**The effect fades.** In the Penn study, the gym boost shrank over the weeks, particularly around holidays when routines broke. Bundling fights present bias, but it doesn't make a habit automatic on its own. Treat it as scaffolding for the early, hardest weeks — the period before [a behaviour becomes automatic](/blog/how-long-to-form-a-habit), which research by Lally and colleagues put at a median of 66 days. Once the habit runs on its own, the bundle has done its job.

**Restriction leakage.** The whole mechanism depends on the indulgence being genuinely unavailable elsewhere. The moment you let yourself listen to the audiobook on the sofa, the gym loses its monopoly on it and the pull collapses. If you can't honestly restrict the want, pick a different want. This is where the locked gym lockers in the study earned their keep — they removed the choice.

**Bundling a bad indulgence.** If the "want" is something genuinely harmful — junk food, doomscrolling that wrecks your mood — you may be reinforcing one problem to fix another. The cleanest bundles use indulgences that are merely unproductive, not destructive. If the indulgence is itself a habit you're trying to break, you're better off in [breaking-bad-habits territory](/blog/breaking-bad-habits) rather than entrenching it.

## A worked example

Say you want to build a stationary-bike habit and you have a true weakness for a particular crime podcast.

The bundle: new episodes of the podcast are *only* allowed on the bike. You delete the app from your phone or, more realistically, you make a personal rule and put the podcast queue on a device you keep by the bike. When a new episode drops, the only way to hear it is to get on.

For the first six to eight weeks, the podcast does the pulling. You'll find yourself looking forward to bike days because that's when you get the episode. Somewhere in that window — assuming you've kept the restriction honest — getting on the bike stops needing the bribe. At that point you can loosen the rule. The bundle was the on-ramp, not the destination.

That arc — borrow appeal early, let automaticity take over later — is how to think about temptation bundling generally. It's a way of buying yourself across the hardest stretch of a new habit, when motivation is unreliable and the payoff is still invisible.

## FAQ

**What is temptation bundling in simple terms?**
It's only letting yourself do something you enjoy while you're doing something you've been avoiding. The audiobook is allowed only at the gym; the show is allowed only while you fold laundry. The pleasure pulls you into the chore, and the restriction is what makes it work.

**Who invented temptation bundling?**
Katherine Milkman, a behavioural economist at the Wharton School, coined the term. She tested it in a 2014 field experiment with Julia Minson and Kevin Volpp, published in *Management Science*, which found that bundling addictive audiobooks with gym access increased gym visits by around 51% in the early weeks.

**How is temptation bundling different from a reward?**
A reward comes after the task and isn't restricted ("I'll watch an episode once I've finished"). A bundle happens during the task, and the indulgence is available *only* during the task. That restriction is the active ingredient — without it, you'll just take the indulgence the easy way.

**Does temptation bundling work long-term?**
Its effect tends to fade, especially when routines are disrupted. It's best used as scaffolding for the first several weeks of a new habit, before the behaviour becomes automatic. Once the habit runs on its own, you can relax the bundle.

**Can I combine temptation bundling with habit stacking?**
Yes, and they complement each other well. Habit stacking gives the behaviour a reliable cue ("after I pour my coffee…"); temptation bundling makes it attractive ("…I drink it only while clearing my inbox"). Together they fix both the starting-signal and the appeal problems at once.
