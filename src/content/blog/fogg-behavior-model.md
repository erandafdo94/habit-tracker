Most advice about building habits quietly assumes the problem is you. You lack willpower, you lack discipline, you lack the right mindset. BJ Fogg, who ran the Behavior Design Lab at Stanford for over two decades, spent his career proving the opposite: behaviour is not a character trait. It's the predictable output of three variables converging at the same moment. Get the variables right and the behaviour happens. Get them wrong and no amount of self-criticism will save you.

That insight is captured in one of the most useful equations in behavioural science: **B = MAP**. Behaviour happens when **M**otivation, **A**bility, and a **P**rompt arrive together. If any one of the three is missing, the behaviour does not occur — full stop. This post walks through each variable, the relationship between them, and how to use the model to engineer habits that stick instead of relying on motivation that doesn't.

## What the Fogg Behavior Model actually says

The model is deceptively simple. For any behaviour to occur, three elements must be present **at the same time**:

- **Motivation** — how much you want to do the behaviour.
- **Ability** — how easy the behaviour is to do.
- **Prompt** — a cue that tells you to do it right now.

Fogg expresses this as B = MAP. The "at the same time" part is the bit people miss. You can be highly motivated to exercise, but if no prompt fires, you'll spend the evening on the sofa. You can have a prompt — your alarm goes off — but if the behaviour is too hard or your motivation has cratered, you'll dismiss it. All three have to land in the same instant.

This reframes failure entirely. When a habit doesn't happen, you're not weak. One of the three variables was missing or too low. That turns a vague problem ("I have no self-control") into a diagnostic one ("which variable failed?"). Diagnosis is something you can act on.

## Motivation: the variable you can't rely on

Motivation is the most intuitive of the three and the least trustworthy. It's the desire to perform a behaviour, and Fogg notes it comes from three sources: sensation (pleasure or pain), anticipation (hope or fear), and belonging (social acceptance or rejection). These are powerful, but they share a fatal flaw — they fluctuate constantly.

Your motivation to go for a run is sky-high on a crisp Sunday morning after a good night's sleep and a motivating podcast. It's near zero on a wet Tuesday when you're tired and behind on work. Because motivation rides this wave, any habit that depends on high motivation will only happen on your best days. The rest of the time the wave is in a trough and the behaviour evaporates.

This is the single most important practical lesson of the model: **don't design habits that require a lot of motivation.** Motivation is a fine catalyst for one-off hard things — signing up for a marathon, having a difficult conversation — but it's a terrible foundation for anything you need to do daily. The athletes and writers and meditators who keep going aren't more motivated than you. They've arranged things so they need less of it.

## Ability: the variable you control most directly

Ability is how easy a behaviour is to do, and it's where the real leverage lies. Fogg's claim is striking: **making a behaviour easier is usually more effective than trying to pump up motivation.** Motivation is hard to manufacture and harder to sustain. Simplicity, by contrast, is something you can engineer deliberately.

Fogg identifies five factors that determine how "hard" a behaviour feels. A behaviour is harder when it costs more of any of these:

| Factor | The behaviour is harder when it… |
|---|---|
| Time | takes longer than you have |
| Money | costs more than you'll spend |
| Physical effort | demands more exertion |
| Mental effort | requires more thinking or decisions |
| Routine | breaks your existing patterns |

Your "ability" for a given behaviour is set by your scarcest resource in that moment. A 45-minute gym session might be trivial in time but expensive in physical effort; reading a dense book might be cheap physically but costly in mental effort. To raise ability, you reduce whichever factor is the bottleneck.

This is exactly why the [two-minute rule](/blog/two-minute-rule) works — it slashes the time and effort cost until the behaviour is almost impossible to refuse. It's why [environment design](/blog/environment-design-habits) is so powerful — putting the running shoes by the door cuts the routine and effort cost of getting started. Every effective tactic in habit formation is, underneath, a way of moving the ability variable up.

## Prompts: the trigger that nothing happens without

You can have abundant motivation and effortless ability, and still nothing happens — because no prompt fired. The prompt is the cue that says *do it now*. Without one, even the easiest, most desirable behaviour just sits there waiting.

Fogg sorts prompts into three types:

1. **Facilitator prompts** — for behaviours you *want* to do but find hard. The prompt is paired with something that raises ability ("here's a one-click way to do the thing you've been meaning to do").
2. **Signal prompts** — for behaviours you're already willing and able to do; they just need a reminder. A sticky note, a phone notification, a calendar ping.
3. **Spark prompts** — for behaviours you *can* do but don't want to. The prompt is paired with motivation (an inspiring message, a reminder of the stakes).

The most reliable prompts aren't notifications at all — they're existing behaviours. Anchoring a new habit to something you already do every day is the core of [habit stacking](/blog/habit-stacking-guide): "after I pour my morning coffee, I'll write one sentence." The established routine becomes the prompt, so you're never relying on remembering. This is also the mechanism behind [implementation intentions](/blog/implementation-intentions) — the "when X, then Y" format is really a way of pre-installing a prompt.

## The action line: how the three variables interact

Here's where the model becomes genuinely predictive. Picture a graph with motivation on the vertical axis and ability on the horizontal axis. Running diagonally across it is what Fogg calls the **action line**. When a prompt fires, the behaviour happens *only if you're above the line*. Below it, the prompt fails.

The curve has a crucial property: motivation and ability **compensate** for each other. A behaviour that's very easy needs almost no motivation to land above the line. A behaviour that's very hard needs enormous motivation. This is why "just try harder" is such poor advice — it asks you to climb the motivation axis, which is steep and slippery, when sliding along the ability axis is far easier.

It also explains a frustrating everyday experience. You set a prompt to do something hard while motivated. Days later the prompt fires again, but your motivation has dropped into a trough, and now you're below the action line. The prompt now feels like an annoyance, so you dismiss it — or worse, you start ignoring that whole class of prompts. Fogg's fix is blunt: if a prompt keeps failing, don't add more prompts and don't berate yourself. **Make the behaviour easier** until it sits below your motivation even on a bad day.

## Putting B=MAP to work: a diagnostic, not a slogan

The real value of the model is as a troubleshooting tool. When a habit isn't happening, run it through the three variables in order:

1. **Is there a prompt?** This is the most common failure and the easiest to miss. If nothing reliably cues the behaviour, fix this first — anchor it to an existing routine rather than hoping you'll remember.
2. **Is it easy enough?** If a prompt fires and you still skip the behaviour, your ability is too low. Shrink it. Cut the time, the steps, the friction. Make it laughably small — Fogg's "floss one tooth" is not a joke, it's the method.
3. **Only then, consider motivation.** If the prompt is solid and the behaviour is genuinely tiny and you *still* won't do it, you may have a motivation problem — or you may not actually want this habit, which is worth knowing.

Notice the order. Most people start with motivation because it's the most visible variable, and it's the one self-help culture obsesses over. Fogg flips the priority: prompt and ability first, motivation last. That ordering is the practical heart of his book [*Tiny Habits*](https://amzn.to/43o1OJe), and it's what makes the model so different from the willpower-centric advice it replaced.

## How the model fits the rest of habit science

B=MAP isn't a competing theory so much as the scaffolding underneath most of the tactics you'll read about elsewhere. Charles Duhigg's cue–routine–reward loop describes how a habit becomes automatic over time; Fogg's model describes the conditions for the behaviour to happen *at all* in the first place. James Clear's four laws — make it obvious, attractive, easy, satisfying — map almost directly onto Fogg's variables: "obvious" is the prompt, "easy" is ability, "attractive" and "satisfying" feed motivation.

If you want behaviours to eventually run on autopilot, you still need repetition and a stable context — that's the realm of [how habits actually form](/blog/how-to-build-habits-that-stick). But you can't repeat a behaviour you never perform, and B=MAP is the model for getting it performed reliably enough that repetition can do its work. Engineer the convergence first; let automaticity follow.

## FAQ

**What does B=MAP stand for?**
Behaviour equals Motivation, Ability, and Prompt. The Fogg Behavior Model states that a behaviour occurs only when all three are present at the same moment. Remove any one and the behaviour doesn't happen, no matter how strong the other two are.

**Which is more important: motivation or ability?**
Ability, in almost every practical case. Motivation fluctuates constantly and is hard to sustain, so habits that depend on it only happen on your best days. Making a behaviour easier is more reliable than trying to manufacture motivation, which is why Fogg advises shrinking the behaviour before anything else.

**How is the Fogg Behavior Model different from Atomic Habits?**
They're complementary. Fogg's B=MAP describes the conditions required for a behaviour to occur in a single moment. James Clear's four laws are a tactical system for designing habits over time, and they map closely onto Fogg's variables — "make it obvious" is the prompt, "make it easy" is ability. Fogg is the underlying model; Clear is one applied toolkit.

**What's a prompt in the Fogg model?**
A prompt is the cue that tells you to do the behaviour right now. It can be a notification, a sticky note, or — most reliably — an existing habit you anchor the new behaviour to. Without a prompt, even an easy, desirable behaviour simply never gets triggered.

**Why do my habit reminders stop working?**
Usually because the behaviour sits above your "action line" on low-motivation days. When a prompt fires but the behaviour is too hard for your current motivation, you dismiss it, and eventually you tune those prompts out. The fix isn't more reminders — it's making the behaviour small enough to do even when motivation is low.
