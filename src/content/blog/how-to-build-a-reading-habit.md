You want to read more, but the book on your bedside table has become furniture. Setting a bigger annual target probably won't solve the problem of opening it tonight. If you want to know how to build a reading habit, start with a repeatable moment and a book you actually want to return to.

## Build the habit of starting, then increase the reading

Reading fifty books is an outcome. Opening your current book after breakfast is an action you can practise. Make that action your first target, and let the number of finished books follow.

In their 2016 review, [Wendy Wood and Dennis Rünger describe habits as responses developed through repetition in recurring contexts](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-122414-033417). Applied to reading, that suggests choosing a recognisable situation in which to begin. It doesn't establish a particular number of pages or minutes that everyone should read.

A useful starting plan is one book, one cue, and a small minimum. For example: after putting your breakfast bowl in the sink, sit in the same chair and read for two minutes. Stop there if necessary. Continue if you have time and want to.

Two minutes is a practical starting suggestion, not a scientifically established threshold. Its purpose is to make the first session manageable on an ordinary busy day. If two minutes feels awkward, try a page or a short poem instead.

The broader [system for building habits that stick](/blog/how-to-build-habits-that-stick) applies here: define the behaviour clearly enough that you know whether you did it. "Read more" gives you an aspiration. "Read one page after breakfast" gives you something to do tomorrow.

Don't add a note-taking system, a reading challenge, and a public review schedule at the same time. Those may become useful later. For now, give yourself one job: begin reading at the moment you chose.

## Choose a book and format you want to return to

Start with interest rather than obligation. A gripping mystery, a graphic novel, a biography, or a collection of essays can all be sensible choices. You don't need to begin with the most demanding book on your shelf to make the habit worthwhile.

Ask a concrete question: would I choose to read the next few pages if nobody knew what I was reading? If the answer is no, consider another book. A reading routine shouldn't require you to defend your taste.

Give an unfamiliar book enough attention to judge it, then decide whether to continue. There is no universal page limit for abandoning a book. Try a chapter or a few short sessions, and distinguish between useful difficulty and simple lack of interest. You can put a book aside without deciding that reading itself isn't for you.

Keep one book as your default while you establish the routine. You can have others available, but decide in advance which one you will open at your usual cue. Otherwise, your two-minute session can disappear into choosing what to read.

Choose the format around the situation. A paperback might suit a chair at home. An e-reader might be easier to carry on a commute. Large print or an audiobook may be more accessible or convenient. These are options to test against your circumstances, not a ranking of legitimate reading.

If your goal is engaging with stories or ideas regularly, listening can be part of your plan. If your goal is practising written-language comprehension or studying a printed text, include that activity specifically. Define the behaviour around what you want to gain.

You also don't need to buy a fresh stack of books. Start with something you own or can borrow. Check that it is available before attaching your routine to it, especially if a library loan or shared device has a deadline.

## Attach reading to a cue you already notice

Pick an event that happens reliably and leaves room for the action. Finishing breakfast is a poor cue if it is immediately followed by a rush to leave. Sitting down on the train may work better if that is when you actually have a few uninterrupted minutes.

Write your plan in one sentence: "After I finish lunch at home, I will sit on the sofa and read one page of my current book."

This borrows from implementation intentions, plans that specify when and how to act. [Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1) found that these plans helped goal attainment across a range of tasks. That supports making a concrete plan; it doesn't guarantee that this particular reading routine will work for you.

The [habit stacking guide](/blog/habit-stacking-guide) explains how to select an existing action as an anchor. For reading, check three things: you notice the cue, your book is available, and you have enough time for your minimum.

If your schedule changes, choose an event that travels with you. Reading after your first meal might be more workable than reading at 7 a.m. You can also keep a backup opportunity, such as after your evening shower, without turning every spare moment into an obligation.

Bedtime is an option, not a requirement. If you routinely fall asleep before finishing a paragraph, move the session earlier. If you read late because you enjoy it, decide when to stop so the routine fits the rest of your evening.

Test one cue for several opportunities before changing it. If you keep missing it, ask what happens at that moment. A cue you never notice or cannot act on needs adjusting; a more emphatic reminder won't create time that isn't there.

## Prepare your space before the reading moment

Before your next session, put the book where you intend to read, with a bookmark at the right place. Arrange enough light and somewhere comfortable to sit. For an e-reader, charge it and download the book in advance.

These are practical ways to apply the principle of repeating an action in a consistent context. The [environment design guide](/blog/environment-design-habits) offers more examples, but your setup can stay modest. You don't need a reading room. You need an available book and a usable place to open it.

Look at the distraction most likely to interrupt those first minutes. If you tend to reach for your phone, try leaving it beyond arm's reach for the session. If you read on your phone, open the book beforehand and silence notifications where practical. Then judge whether that setup helps you complete the session.

Avoid making preparation a larger project than reading. Buying a new chair, reorganising your shelves, and researching e-readers can all wait. Set up what you already have, then use it.

For nonfiction, decide whether this is a reading session or a study session. You might read a few pages now and return later to make notes. If a passage deserves attention, mark it or write one sentence about it. You can choose a more detailed method when the material calls for it.

Before closing the book, leave the next start ready. Replace the bookmark, return the book to its place, and check that it will be there tomorrow. Preparation is most useful when it removes a specific obstacle you've actually encountered.

## Try a two-week plan and review what happened

Use the following plan as an experiment. Two weeks gives you several opportunities to notice problems; it is not a deadline for reading to become automatic.

| Period | What to practise | What to review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before starting | Choose your book, cue, place, and minimum | Is everything available at that moment? |
| Days 1-3 | Read your minimum after the cue | Did you notice the cue and begin? |
| Days 4-7 | Repeat the same plan | What interrupted or delayed the start? |
| Days 8-14 | Keep the minimum; extend sessions if you want | Is the routine practical and the book engaging? |
| After two weeks | Keep or adjust the plan | Which single change would help most? |

Mark a completion after each session. A tick on a calendar is enough. Record the behaviour you intended to establish, such as starting after breakfast, rather than building a spreadsheet you don't want to maintain.

If you read longer, you can note the minutes too. Keep the distinction clear: the minimum tells you whether you followed your plan; extra reading tells you how much time you spent. Neither requires comparing yourself with somebody else's annual book count.

Review the pattern at the end of each week. If you began consistently and enjoyed continuing, try a slightly longer usual session while keeping a smaller fallback for busy days. If you rarely started, leave the target small and adjust the cue or setting first.

When you miss a session, return at the next planned opportunity. Don't assign yourself double the pages as repayment. In [Phillippa Lally and colleagues' habit-formation study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674), missing one opportunity did not materially affect the formation process. The study examined everyday eating, drinking, and activity behaviours, so this is a useful general finding rather than a reading-specific result.

For a longer interruption, use the [habit restart approach](/blog/how-to-restart-a-habit): return to a manageable version and check whether your original cue still fits. Travel, a new job, or caring responsibilities may require a different plan.

Don't treat a calendar date as proof that the habit should now feel effortless. Lally's study also found substantial variation in the time course of automaticity. For your reading routine, ask whether you begin more readily and whether the plan fits your life. Those are more useful observations than declaring yourself behind schedule.

## FAQ

**How many pages should I read a day to build a reading habit?**

Start with a minimum you can manage on a busy day, such as one page or two minutes. Page counts vary with the book's layout and difficulty, so choose the measure that is easiest to use. Increase your usual session when you want more reading time, while keeping a small fallback available.

**How long does it take to build a reading habit?**

There isn't a universal reading-specific deadline established by the research cited here. Use your first two weeks to test the routine, then keep reviewing it. Notice whether you start more readily at your cue rather than expecting automaticity after a fixed number of days.

**Is it better to read in the morning or at night?**

Choose the time when you can reliably give the book your attention. Try mornings if you have a quiet interval, or evenings if that is when time opens up. If your chosen slot repeatedly fails, move it. You don't need to copy someone else's schedule.

**Do audiobooks count towards a reading habit?**

They can count if your goal is regularly engaging with books through listening. If your goal involves practising reading written text, make room for that specifically. Choose a definition that serves your purpose, then track the activity honestly rather than debating whether one format is superior.

**What should I do if I keep abandoning books?**

Check the book choice and the routine separately. Try something that interests you immediately, then use a manageable cue and session. If you enjoy reading once you begin, work on the start. If you begin reliably but dislike the book, change the book. Adjust one part at a time so you can see what helps.
