Staring at a blank page trying to invent your own "after X, I will Y" formula is the single biggest reason people give up on habit stacking before they start. You don't need to invent one. You need to steal one that already fits an anchor you already have, and adjust it until it's specific enough to survive a bad day.

This is a working list, not a lecture. If you want the mechanism behind why habit stacking works — the research, the rules for picking a good anchor, the failure modes — read the [full habit stacking guide](/blog/habit-stacking-guide) first. Here, we're skipping straight to formulas you can copy in the next sixty seconds.

## The formula, in one line

Every example below follows the same shape, first popularised by BJ Fogg's "tiny habits" work and formalised by James Clear as habit stacking:

**After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT].**

The current habit is your anchor — something you already do automatically, every day, at a specific point in your routine. The new habit rides on its coattails. No motivation required, because you're not starting from zero; you're starting from a cue that already fires reliably.

Two rules make the difference between a stack that sticks and one you forget by Thursday:

1. **The anchor has to be rock-solid.** "After I wake up" is a weak anchor because waking up isn't a discrete, observable action. "After I turn off my alarm" is a strong one.
2. **The new habit has to be small enough to survive your worst day.** If the stacked habit takes longer than two minutes, shrink it. You can always do more once it's automatic — see the [two-minute rule](/blog/two-minute-rule) for why starting small is the whole point, not a compromise.

With that out of the way, here are the stacks, organised by the part of the day you'll attach them to.

## Morning habit stacking examples

Mornings are the easiest place to start because most people already have 3–5 fixed anchors before they've even left the bedroom.

1. After I turn off my alarm, I will put both feet on the floor before picking up my phone.
2. After I put both feet on the floor, I will drink the glass of water I left on my nightstand.
3. After I brush my teeth, I will do 10 bodyweight squats.
4. After I make my coffee, I will write down the one thing that would make today a win.
5. After I sit down with my coffee, I will read one page of a book instead of opening a news app.
6. After I get dressed, I will make my bed.
7. After I make my bed, I will open the curtains and get two minutes of natural light.
8. After I feed the dog/cat, I will stretch for one minute.
9. After I pour my cereal, I will take my vitamins or medication.
10. After I finish breakfast, I will pack my bag for the day ahead so I'm not scrambling later.

If you're building a whole sequence rather than one link, see the [evening routine habits](/blog/evening-routine-habits) piece for how the same logic works in reverse to protect tomorrow morning.

## Work and daytime habit stacking examples

Daytime anchors are less predictable than morning ones, which is exactly why they're often skipped. Pick anchors tied to transitions — sitting down, opening an app, ending a call — rather than vague blocks of time like "sometime in the afternoon."

11. After I sit down at my desk, I will write my top three priorities for the day.
12. After I open my laptop, I will close every tab that isn't related to my first task.
13. After I finish a meeting, I will write down one action item before opening anything else.
14. After I send a difficult email, I will stand up and take a 60-second walk.
15. After my lunch break ends, I will do a two-minute breathing reset before returning to work.
16. After I check off my top priority, I will reward myself with five minutes of something enjoyable.
17. After I get a notification urge to check social media, I will take three breaths before deciding whether to open the app.
18. After I close my laptop for the day, I will write tomorrow's top priority so I don't start the next day blank.
19. After I finish my last work call, I will physically leave my desk, even if just for two minutes.
20. After I clock out, I will change out of "work clothes" to signal the day is over.

This kind of transition-based stacking is closest to what researchers call an implementation intention — see [implementation intentions](/blog/implementation-intentions) for the underlying if-then mechanism and why it roughly doubles follow-through in controlled studies.

## Evening habit stacking examples

Evenings fail more often than mornings because willpower is depleted and screens are everywhere. Strong anchors matter more here, not less.

21. After I put my plate in the sink, I will wipe down one kitchen surface.
22. After dinner is finished, I will put my phone on charge outside the bedroom.
23. After I lock the front door, I will lay out clothes for tomorrow.
24. After I brush my teeth at night, I will write down one thing that went well today.
25. After I get into bed, I will read one page instead of scrolling.
26. After I turn off the TV, I will do a two-minute tidy of the room I'm sitting in.
27. After I set tomorrow's alarm, I will put my phone face-down and out of reach.
28. After I finish my nighttime skincare or routine, I will do a 60-second stretch.
29. After I turn off the lights, I will do one slow breath cycle before closing my eyes.

## Health and fitness habit stacking examples

These work best when stacked onto something you already do near where the new habit needs to happen — by your gym bag, your kettle, your car keys.

30. After I fill the kettle, I will do 10 calf raises while it boils.
31. After I finish a shower, I will do a 30-second cold-water finish.
32. After I park the car at home, I will walk one extra loop of the block before going inside.
33. After I take my dog out, I will do five minutes of extra walking beyond the usual route.
34. After I finish a meal, I will leave the table instead of continuing to snack.
35. After I pour a glass of water, I will drink half of it immediately instead of sipping over an hour.
36. After I put on my gym shoes, I will commit to just the first five minutes of the workout.
37. After I sit down to watch TV, I will do a set of stretches during the first ad break or intro.
38. After I take my morning medication, I will log my weight or a health metric you're tracking.

## Money and admin habit stacking examples

Financial habits die from lack of a trigger more than lack of intention. These anchors borrow from actions you already take around money.

39. After I get paid, I will move a fixed amount to savings before I see the rest of the balance.
40. After I buy something over $50, I will log it in a spending tracker the same day.
41. After I open my banking app, I will glance at my savings goal before checking my main balance.
42. After I pay a recurring bill, I will check whether it's still worth the cost.
43. After I finish my Sunday grocery shop, I will do a five-minute review of the week's spending.
44. After I get a receipt, I will photograph it before it goes in my pocket or bin.

## Relationship and connection habit stacking examples

These are the easiest to forget and the highest-leverage to keep, because the anchor is often a habit you already share with someone else.

45. After I sit down for dinner, I will ask one specific question instead of "how was your day."
46. After I finish my commute home, I will put my phone away for the first ten minutes indoors.
47. After I put the kids to bed, I will send one message to a friend I haven't spoken to in a while.
48. After my weekly one-on-one ends, I will note one thing to follow up on with that person.
49. After I finish a call with a parent or old friend, I will schedule the next one before hanging up.

## How to pick the right example for you

Don't adopt more than one or two of these at once. Stacking five new habits onto five different anchors in the same week is how people burn out and quit the whole system within ten days — the opposite of what habit stacking is supposed to prevent. Pick the single anchor in your day that's most reliable, attach one two-minute habit to it, and let it run for two to three weeks before adding a second.

If an example above doesn't fit your actual routine, don't force it — swap the anchor for something you genuinely do every day without fail. A stack only works if the first link in the chain is unbreakable. For the underlying reasoning on how long that automaticity actually takes to kick in, see [how long it takes to form a habit](/blog/how-long-to-form-a-habit) — it's longer than the popular "21 days" myth suggests, and knowing the real number will stop you from quitting a stack that's actually working.

## FAQ

**How many habits should I stack at once?**
Start with one. Once it runs on autopilot for two to three weeks, add a second. Stacking several new habits simultaneously overloads the same limited attention and willpower that habit stacking is designed to route around.

**What if my anchor habit isn't consistent every day?**
Pick a different anchor. The entire mechanism depends on the anchor firing reliably; an inconsistent trigger produces an inconsistent habit. Weekday-only anchors (like "after I park at the office") are fine as long as you're honest that the stack only applies on those days.

**Can I stack a habit onto another habit I'm still building?**
Not yet. Stack new habits onto things that are already fully automatic — ideally habits you've done the same way for months. Stacking onto a fragile habit means if the first one slips, the second one has nothing to hold onto.

**Do habit stacking examples work the same for breaking bad habits?**
Partially. Stacking is best for adding a new behaviour after an existing cue. For removing an unwanted habit, you generally need to interrupt or replace the cue-routine-reward loop itself — see [how to break a bad habit](/blog/breaking-bad-habits) for that different approach.

**What's the difference between habit stacking and an implementation intention?**
They're close cousins. An implementation intention is any "if X, then I will Y" plan, which can use a time, location, or emotional cue as the trigger. Habit stacking is a specific, narrower version that always uses an existing habit as the trigger. Every habit stack is an implementation intention, but not every implementation intention is a habit stack.
